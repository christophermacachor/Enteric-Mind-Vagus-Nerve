1. Introduction: The Cortico-Centric Error and Its Convergence Crisis

Neuroscience has operated for over a century under what we term the cortico-centric error: the assumption that consciousness, emotion, empathy, and social cognition originate in the cerebral cortex. This assumption is not empirically grounded in evolutionary biology. The enteric nervous system (ENS) is approximately 600 million years old; the cerebral cortex is approximately 250 million years old. The ENS contains ~2×10⁸ neurons, operates independently of the central nervous system, and generates over 90% of the body's serotonin and approximately 50% of its dopamine (Furness, 2012; Gershon, 1998; Spencer & Hu, 2020).

Two independent developments in 2025 have rendered this cortico-centrism untenable.

First, Silva, Lemoine, Rera, and Hadjieconomou (2025), building on the "first brain" framing established by Furness and Stebbing (2018), synthesize comparative evidence from early-diverging metazoans through Drosophila and vertebrates to demonstrate that gut-embedded neural circuits arose on an epithelial/peptidergic scaffold and evolved in parallel across lineages, coordinating motility and secretion before centralized brains emerged. These authors formalize the intestine, through its neuronal, epithelial, and immune components, as an Enterarchon: an ancient visceral brain shaping organismal homeostasis. This biological primacy invites a fundamental ontological question: if the enteric substrate predates the cortex and operates independently, what is the functional relationship between enteric and cortical consciousness?

Second, Martial et al. (2025), reviewing NDE neurophysiology in Nature Reviews Neurology, acknowledge that rich, coherent conscious experience persists during conditions of cortical failure—flat EEG, absent brainstem reflexes, and no cerebral blood flow. They describe this as "disconnected consciousness" and explicitly leave open the question of how consciousness emerges under such conditions.

These two findings—gut primacy from comparative biology and consciousness persistence beyond cortex from clinical neuroscience—converge on a single inference: the cortex is not the generator of consciousness but a filter that constrains it. When the filter fails (NDE) or when the primary substrate is liberated (enteric modification), the underlying scalar field becomes perceptible.

We formalize this inference as the Enterarchon Hypothesis: the ENS functions as the primary receptor for a universal scalar coherence field; the cortex evolved as a localized dampener enabling vectorial processing (spatial navigation, symbolic abstraction, executive control) at the cost of scalar fidelity; and clinical conditions currently classified as neurological, psychiatric, or "functional" gastrointestinal disorders may represent disturbances in this density-differential architecture.

1. Hypoxia typically produces confusion and fragmentation, not hyper-coherent mystical states. NDEs are structured, cross-culturally consistent, and narratively organized—unlike hypoxic delirium.
2. 5-HT2A hyperactivation (psychedelic model) produces ego dissolution and unstructured visionary content, whereas NDEs preserve a coherent narrative self, life review, and encounter with deceased relatives.
3. Cross-cultural invariance contradicts the "cultural expectation" hypothesis. If NDEs were constructed by top-down psychological processes, massive variation should exist; instead, core features (out-of-body perception, tunnel/light, life review, being encounter, return) are remarkably invariant.
4. Evolutionary threat response should favor terror and fight-or-flight mobilization, not the profound peace and surrender characteristic of NDEs.
