1. Introduction

Modern gastroenterology faces a fundamental paradox. Approximately 40% of 
patients presenting with gastrointestinal symptoms receive diagnoses of 
"functional" disorders — now termed disorders of gut-brain interaction 
(DGBI) — because available assessments fail to identify organic pathology 
(Chang, 2004; Talley, 2008). These patients are frequently referred to 
psychiatrists or psychologists, implying that their suffering originates in 
the mind rather than the gut (Porges, 2025a). This symptom-based model, 
rooted in nosological tradition, prioritizes symptom relief over mechanistic 
understanding and leaves the root causes of DGBI unaddressed.

Porges (2025a,b) has advanced a compelling alternative. Through polyvagal 
theory (PVT), he reframes DGBI not as psychological dysfunction but as 
disorders of neural regulation — specifically, dysregulation of the 
brainstem circuits that coordinate bidirectional vagus nerve communication 
between visceral organs and the central nervous system. His recent 
introduction of vagal efficiency (VE) — a metric quantifying the dynamic 
relationship between respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA) and heart period 
during physiological challenge — provides the first brainstem-level 
biomarker capable of discriminating DGBI subtypes (Porges, 2025b).

We propose that Porges' framework, while revolutionary, remains incomplete. 
PVT describes the brainstem bridge between gut and behavior but does not 
address the enteric nervous system (ENS) as the origin of the signals that 
cross that bridge. The ENS contains approximately 2×10⁸ neurons (Furness, 
2012; Spencer & Hu, 2020), operates independently of the central nervous 
system (Gershon, 1998), and has existed for 600 million years — predating 
the vertebrate brain by hundreds of millions of years. We argue that the 
ENS is not merely a digestive regulator but the primary density well for 
what we term scalar consciousness: a biological coherence field that 
mediates empathy, intuition, and social connection.

This manuscript presents the Macachor Scalar Convergence Theorem — a 
formal theoretical framework integrating ENS neurobiology, polyvagal 
theory, and clinical phenomenology. We introduce eight theorems, ten 
corollaries, and a clinical research framework with six study designs. 
We operationalize vagal efficiency as the magnitude response of the 
Aperture Transfer Function |H_p(ω)| — the quantitative link between 
enteric density detection and cardiac vagal output. We present five 
falsifiable predictions and a first-person case study from a GI 
hemorrhage survivor.

6.1 Vagal Efficiency

Primary outcome measure: VE via Porges-Bohrer method.
Protocol: Continuous ECG and respiration recording during 5-minute supine
